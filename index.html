📊 Quick Reference — The Variable Matrix

The answer

Ask yourself three fast questions in order:

1. What am I being asked to do? Compare groups → ANOVA or Chi-Square. Find a relationship between two measured things → Regression. Check if a distribution matches a claim → GOF.

2. What type are my variables? Categorical (groups, labels, yes/no) vs. Quantitative (measured numbers). Two categorical → Chi-Square. One categorical + one quantitative → ANOVA. Two quantitative → Regression.

3. How many groups? If it's one categorical grouping variable + one quantitative response, count the groups. Three or more → ANOVA. Two → Two-sample t-test (Unit 3).

🧠 The one-sentence version: "Is it categorical vs. categorical? → Chi-Square. Categorical vs. quantitative (3+ groups)? → ANOVA. Quantitative vs. quantitative? → Regression."

The answer

The clearest distinguishing question: How many categorical variables am I working with?

One variable → Goodness-of-Fit. You have a single categorical variable (e.g., "favorite color") and you are asking whether the distribution of responses in your sample matches a specific claimed distribution (e.g., "each color is equally preferred"). The comparison is observed counts vs. expected counts derived from a stated percentages or proportions.

Two variables → Test for Independence. You have two categorical variables (e.g., "gender" and "preferred study method") and you are asking whether knowing one variable tells you anything about the other. The data lives in a contingency table with rows and columns.

🧠 The one-word test: If your data can be summarized in a simple list of counts for one category → GOF. If your data requires a table with rows AND columns → Independence.
Example — GOF: A professor claims 30% of students prefer morning classes, 50% afternoon, and 20% evening. You survey 200 students. One variable (time preference), comparing to claimed percentages → Chi-Square GOF.

Example — Independence: You survey students and record both their time preference AND their GPA category (below/above 3.0). Two variables → Chi-Square Test for Independence.
⚠️ Common mistake: Running a GOF test because you see percentage claims in the problem — but if the problem presents a contingency table (with rows and columns), it is always Independence, not GOF, even if percentages are mentioned.

The answer

You are right that the calculation is identical. The difference is in the research design — specifically, how the data was collected.

Test for Independence: You drew one random sample from a single population and measured two categorical variables on every person. You are asking: are these two variables related within this population? Example: survey 300 SAC students and record both their major and their preferred study location. One sample, two variables.

Test for Homogeneity: You drew separate random samples from two or more distinct populations and recorded the same categorical variable in each. You are asking: do the distributions of this variable look the same across all populations? Example: separately survey 150 freshmen, 150 sophomores, and 150 juniors about their preferred study location. Three separate samples, one variable.

🧠 The key question: "Did I draw one sample and measure two things, or did I draw multiple separate samples?" One sample → Independence. Multiple separate samples → Homogeneity.
Practical note: In many real-world problems — and on your homework — the distinction between these two is not tested at the calculation level, since the math is identical. What matters is that you can state the correct hypotheses and write an accurate contextual conclusion. Independence concludes about association; Homogeneity concludes about equal distributions across groups.

The answer

Each hypothesis test at α = 0.05 carries a 5% chance of a Type I error — falsely rejecting a true null hypothesis. When you run just one test, that risk is 5%. But each additional test multiplies the cumulative risk of making at least one false positive.

With three groups (A, B, C), you would need three separate t-tests: A vs. B, A vs. C, and B vs. C. The probability of making at least one error across those three tests rises to approximately 14%. With four groups (six comparisons), it climbs past 26%.

ANOVA runs all comparisons in a single test, keeping the Type I error rate controlled at exactly α = 0.05 across the entire analysis.

The core principle: One test, one alpha. Multiple tests, compounding errors. ANOVA protects you from "fishing" for significance by limiting the family-wise error rate to α.
⚠️ Common mistake: Thinking that because each individual t-test uses α = 0.05, the combined analysis is still at 5% risk. Each test is an independent gamble. The more tests you run, the more likely you eventually roll the wrong number — even when H₀ is actually true for all groups.

The answer

Technically, yes — ANOVA can handle two groups. In fact, when you have exactly two groups, the F-statistic from ANOVA equals the square of the t-statistic from a two-sample t-test (F = t²), and both tests produce identical p-values. They are mathematically the same test in disguise.

However, by convention — and for this course — use a two-sample t-test when comparing exactly two groups, and ANOVA when comparing three or more groups. The two-sample t-test also has the advantage of giving you confidence intervals for the difference in means, which ANOVA alone does not.

🧠 Simple convention: 2 groups → Two-sample t-test (Unit 3). 3+ groups → ANOVA. The math converges at 2 groups, but the convention is clear.
Connection to Unit 3: If you see a problem with two quantitative groups and a quantitative response, that is the two-sample t-test you already know. ANOVA becomes necessary only when a third (or more) group is added to the comparison.

Conducting Tests & Technology

S22 · 12 questions

The answer

They are similar in form but different in meaning. Both are always non-directional (no >, <, only "does not fit" or "is associated").

Goodness-of-Fit:
H₀: The distribution of [variable] fits (matches) the claimed distribution.
Hₐ: The distribution of [variable] does not fit the claimed distribution.

Test for Independence:
H₀: [Variable A] and [Variable B] are independent (not associated).
Hₐ: [Variable A] and [Variable B] are not independent (they are associated).

🧠 Memory anchor: The null hypothesis always assumes nothing interesting is happening — for GOF, the "nothing" is that the data fits the claim. For Independence, the "nothing" is that the two variables have no relationship at all.
Key difference: Notice that there is no directionality in Chi-Square hypotheses. You never write H₀: p₁ > p₂ or anything like that. Chi-Square is always about "fit or don't fit" and "associated or not associated."
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing Hₐ as "variable A affects variable B." The Chi-Square test cannot establish causation or direction — only whether an association exists. Always write Hₐ as "are associated" or "are not independent," never "causes."

The answer

H₀: All group means are equal. (μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = … = μₖ)

Hₐ: At least one group mean differs from the others.

The reason Hₐ says "at least one" (not "all are different") is important. ANOVA is an omnibus test — it detects whether any difference exists somewhere among the groups. It does not tell you which groups differ or how many. If you reject H₀, all you know is that the group means are not all the same. Some might be identical to each other while one is very different — ANOVA still flags it.

Why this matters for conclusions: When you reject H₀ in ANOVA, you cannot write "all group means are significantly different." You must write: "There is significant evidence that at least one group mean differs from the others." A post-hoc test (like Tukey's) would be needed to specify which pairs differ.
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing H₀ as "the group means are different" (backwards!) or Hₐ as "all group means are different from each other." ANOVA's alternative is much more modest — just one outlier mean is enough to reject H₀.

The answer

H₀ is always the same: β = 0 (the slope is zero — no linear relationship exists).

The choice between two-tailed and one-tailed depends on what the research question is asking:

Two-tailed (β ≠ 0): Use when the question asks "Is there a linear relationship?" or "Does a correlation exist?" without specifying direction. This is the most common case in this course. You are simply asking whether the slope is meaningfully different from flat.

One-tailed (β > 0 or β < 0): Use only when the question explicitly predicts the direction of the relationship before collecting data. For example: "Does spending more time studying lead to higher exam scores?" predicts a positive slope → Hₐ: β > 0. Look for prior-directional language like "leads to," "increases with," "as X rises, Y also rises."

🧠 When in doubt, use two-tailed (β ≠ 0). In practice and in this course, most regression tests are two-tailed. Use one-tailed only when the problem clearly predicts direction before seeing the data — not just because your scatterplot happens to show a positive slope.
⚠️ Common mistake: Looking at the scatterplot, seeing a positive slope, and writing Hₐ: β > 0. The direction of your alternative hypothesis should be determined by the research question and prior reasoning — not by peeking at the data you already collected.

The answer

The F-statistic is a ratio that compares two sources of variability in your data:

F = (variability between groups) ÷ (variability within groups)

Think of it this way: if the group means are truly different, then how much the group averages spread out (between-group variability) should be large relative to how much individual values bounce around within each group (within-group variability). A large F-ratio → the signal (between groups) is loud relative to the noise (within groups) → evidence that at least one group mean is different.

If H₀ is true (all means equal), the between-group variability should be roughly as large as the within-group variability → F ≈ 1. Large F-values → small p-value → reject H₀.

🧠 Intuition: F is a signal-to-noise ratio. "Is the spread between my group averages bigger than the spread inside my groups?" If yes → something real is going on between the groups.
Reading Art of Stat output: The ANOVA table gives you the F-statistic directly. Use it alongside the p-value for your decision. You do not need to compute F by hand — but you do need to know what it is conceptually to write a meaningful conclusion.

The answer

Here is the complete framework in two parts:

The decision: Compare your p-value to α = 0.05.
p-value < 0.05 → Reject H₀ (the data is surprising under H₀ — something is going on)
p-value ≥ 0.05 → Fail to Reject H₀ (the data is not surprising enough to doubt H₀)

The conclusion sentence:
Reject H₀ → "There IS sufficient evidence to support the claim that [Hₐ in context]."
Fail to Reject → "There is NOT sufficient evidence to support the claim that [Hₐ in context]."

🎵 Memory rhyme: "If the p is low, the null must go!" Small p-value = reject = sufficient evidence.
🧠 Context is required: "Reject H₀" alone is never a complete conclusion. You must always follow it with what that decision means in plain language. "There is sufficient evidence to support the claim that study method and exam outcome are associated among STAT C1000 students at SAC."
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing "we accept H₀" when you fail to reject. We never "accept" — we simply lack sufficient evidence against it. Also: saying "we proved" anything. Statistics deals in evidence, not proof.

The answer

The p-value tells you: if the two variables were truly independent (H₀ true), how likely is it to see discrepancies between observed and expected counts as large as (or larger than) what we actually observed?

A small p-value means: the gap between what we observed and what H₀ predicted is too large to be explained by chance alone. The variables are likely associated.

A large p-value means: the gap between observed and expected is the kind of gap we would expect just from random sampling variation. No convincing evidence of association.

Try this sentence structure for your conclusion: "Because the p-value [value] is [less/greater] than α = 0.05, we [reject/fail to reject] H₀. There [is/is not] sufficient evidence to conclude that [Variable A] and [Variable B] are [associated/independent] among [population]."
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing "the p-value shows the variables are related." The p-value doesn't directly show the relationship — it measures the strength of evidence against the null hypothesis. The conclusion about "related" or "not related" is your inference from that evidence.

The answer

Use the 2:1 rule of thumb: the equal variance assumption is reasonably satisfied if the largest sample standard deviation is less than twice the smallest sample standard deviation.

In symbols: smax / smin < 2

To check: find the largest s and the smallest s among all your groups. Divide. If the ratio is less than 2, proceed with ANOVA. If it is 2 or greater, the equal variance assumption may be violated and results should be interpreted cautiously.

Example: Three groups with s = 4.1, 5.8, and 7.3. Largest/smallest = 7.3 / 4.1 ≈ 1.78 < 2 → assumption is met.
Additional ANOVA conditions to verify: (1) Each group is a random sample from its population. (2) The groups are independent of each other. (3) Each population is approximately normally distributed OR each sample size is large enough (n ≥ 30 per group) for the CLT to apply. (4) Equal variance (the 2:1 rule).

The answer

Use the "Contingency Table" option under Enter Data. This option accepts r × c tables — meaning it supports any number of rows and columns, which matches real homework and exam problems that often have 3 or more columns.

The "Individual Observations" option is designed for raw data entry (each row is one person's data) and is limited in its contingency table configuration — avoid it for Independence tests when your problem already gives you a table of counts.

The "From Textbook" option also works if the exact textbook table is available in the tool's dropdown, but "Contingency Table" is the most reliable general option.

🧠 Quick check: Does your data look like a table with row labels, column labels, and cell counts? → Use Contingency Table. Do you have a list of individual observations? → Use Individual Observations.

The answer

Observed counts (O): These are the actual counts from your data — the numbers you collected and recorded. They come directly from your sample. In a contingency table, observed counts fill each cell.

Expected counts (E): These are the counts you would expect to see if H₀ were true — i.e., if the variables were perfectly independent or if the distribution perfectly matched the claim.

For GOF: E = (claimed percentage) × (total sample size). Example: If 30% is claimed and n = 200, then E = 0.30 × 200 = 60.

For Independence: E = (row total × column total) / grand total for each cell.

The Chi-Square statistic measures how far the observed counts stray from expected: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E. Large discrepancies → large χ² → small p-value → reject H₀.

🧠 Memory tip: "O is what you've GOT. E is what you'd GET if H₀ were true." The test measures the gap between what you got and what H₀ predicts you should get.
⚠️ Common mistake: Using the expected percentages directly (like 0.30) instead of computing the expected count (0.30 × n). The chi-square formula requires counts, not percentages. Always multiply the percentage by the sample size first.

The answer

A prediction is valid when the x-value you are plugging in falls within the range of x-values in your original dataset. Extrapolation occurs when you plug in an x-value that is outside that range — above the maximum or below the minimum x in your data.

Beyond the data range, we have no evidence that the linear relationship continues to hold. The relationship might curve, flatten, reverse, or become undefined. Any prediction there is speculation, not statistical inference.

From your Art of Stat regression output, identify: What is the smallest x-value in your data? What is the largest? Your prediction x must fall between those two values to be considered valid interpolation.

Three conditions to justify using regression for prediction: (1) The p-value for the slope is significant (p < 0.05) — confirming a linear relationship exists. (2) r² is reasonably large — meaning X is a useful predictor of Y. (3) The x-value you are predicting at is within the original data range (not extrapolation).
⚠️ Common mistake: Assuming that a significant p-value alone means the prediction is reliable. A p-value tells you a linear relationship exists, but a low r² means X explains very little of the variation in Y — so the prediction will have very wide uncertainty, even if technically valid.

The answer

Boxplots are supporting evidence, not the test itself. The p-value from ANOVA is your actual decision-maker. Here is how to use boxplots correctly:

Before the test: Examine boxplots to check assumptions — do the spreads (IQR) look roughly similar across groups? Are there obvious outliers? Does the data look approximately symmetric (or are samples large enough for CLT)?

After the test: If you reject H₀, use boxplots to reason about which groups might be driving the difference — look for a group whose median and spread are clearly separated from the others. Write: "The boxplot suggests Group X may have a notably higher median than Groups Y and Z, which is consistent with our ANOVA finding."

Avoid: Do not say "the boxplots show we should reject H₀" — boxplot overlap is not a statistical test. Overlapping boxes do not guarantee fail to reject, and separated boxes do not guarantee reject. The p-value decides.

🧠 Boxplot role: Assumption checker before the test, visual support after the test. Never a substitute for the p-value decision.

The answer

In this course, ANOVA is an omnibus test — it can only tell you that at least one mean differs. Without a post-hoc test (like Tukey's HSD), we cannot make a definitive statistical claim about which specific pairs are different.

However, when homework asks you to reason about group differences informally, use this approach:

1. List the sample means for each group from your output.
2. Examine the boxplots — which group's box is clearly separated from the others?
3. Write a qualified, tentative statement: "The sample data suggests that Group X (mean = ___) may differ most from Group Y (mean = ___), though a post-hoc test would be needed to confirm this statistically."

Important limitation: Never write "ANOVA shows that Group A and Group B are different" without a post-hoc test. ANOVA alone does not authorize that conclusion. ANOVA's reject decision means "at least one differs" — that is all.
🧠 Analogy: ANOVA is like a smoke detector — it tells you there is fire somewhere in the building. It does not tell you which room. A post-hoc test is the firefighter who searches each room to find exactly where.

The answer

The visual assessment is inherently somewhat subjective — two reasonable statisticians can disagree about whether a scatterplot is "moderate" or "strong." That is a real limitation of visual analysis. The solution is to anchor your description to the numerical correlation coefficient r.

Approximate correlation strength guidelines (using |r|)
|r| valueVerbal strengthWhat it looks like
0.8 – 1.0StrongPoints cluster very tightly around the line; the line is clearly visible as a pattern
0.5 – 0.79ModerateNoticeable linear trend, but points scatter with meaningful spread around the line
0.3 – 0.49WeakLinear trend barely visible; wide scatter dominates the picture
0 – 0.29Very weak / noneNo discernible linear trend; looks like a cloud
🧠 Use r to anchor visual language: Instead of "I think it looks strong," write "The correlation coefficient r = 0.86 indicates a strong positive linear association." Let the number drive the description.
⚠️ Common mistake: Describing a relationship as strong simply because the slope is steep. A steep slope has nothing to do with strength — strength is about how tightly points cluster around the line. A scatter with a steep slope but wide spread still has a weak or moderate r.

The answer

Yes — absolutely. Slope and r² measure fundamentally different things and can diverge dramatically.

Slope (b) tells you the rate of change: for each one-unit increase in X, how much does Y change on average? A steep slope just means a large numerical change — it says nothing about how reliable that prediction is.

r² (coefficient of determination) tells you how much of the variation in Y is explained by X. An r² of 0.12 means X explains only 12% of the variation in Y — even if the slope is large.

Imagine plotting shoe size (X) vs. salary (Y) for a random sample of adults. Shoe size is slightly correlated with height, and taller people tend to earn slightly more — so there might be a non-zero slope. But shoe size explains almost none of the variation in salary (r² near zero). A steep-looking slope with terrible predictive power.

Key insight: Slope = "how fast." r² = "how reliably." You can have a fast (steep) but unreliable (weak r²) relationship — or a slow (shallow) but reliable (strong r²) relationship. Always report both for a complete picture.
🧠 r² interpretation template: "Approximately [r² × 100]% of the variation in [Y variable] can be explained by the linear relationship with [X variable]." The remaining [100 − r²]% is explained by other factors or random variation.

The answer

Use this fill-in template:

"For every one [unit of X] increase in [X variable name], we predict [Y variable name] to [increase / decrease] by [|b|] [unit of Y], on average."

Determine increase vs. decrease from the sign of b: positive b = increase, negative b = decrease.

Example: Regression equation: ŷ = 12.5 + 4.83x, where x = hours studied and y = exam score.
"For every one additional hour studied, we predict the exam score to increase by 4.83 points, on average."

🧠 Three things to always include: (1) The direction (increases or decreases). (2) The amount (the numerical value of b). (3) The units of BOTH variables. Without units, the interpretation is incomplete.
⚠️ Common mistakes: (1) Forgetting "on average" — the slope is a prediction, not a certainty. Every individual point will deviate from the line. (2) Confusing which variable is X and which is Y when reading the slope. (3) Dropping the units.

The answer

Use this fill-in template:

"When [X variable] = 0, we predict [Y variable] to be [a] [unit of Y]."

However — before you write this, ask: Is X = 0 a reasonable value within my data context?

If X = 0 is impossible or outside the data range, the y-intercept is not meaningful in context — it's just a mathematical anchor for the line. In that case, simply report the value and note: "The y-intercept of [a] is not meaningful here because [X variable] = 0 is not a realistic or observed scenario in this dataset."

Example — meaningful y-intercept: ŷ = 50 + 3.2x, where x = years of experience and y = salary (in thousands). "When years of experience = 0, we predict a starting salary of $50,000." X = 0 is realistic (a new hire) → interpretation is valid.

Example — not meaningful: ŷ = −25 + 1.4x, where x = daily steps and y = weight (lbs). "When steps = 0, predicted weight is −25 lbs." This is nonsensical → note the y-intercept is mathematically necessary but contextually uninterpretable.

The answer

Ask this question: "Which variable would a researcher naturally use to try to predict or explain the other?"

X = explanatory variable (also called predictor, independent variable). This is the variable the researcher controls, manipulates, or uses as the input. It is measured first or makes causal/theoretical sense as the "cause."

Y = response variable (also called outcome, dependent variable). This is what you are trying to predict, measure the effect on, or explain. It is the output.

🧠 The "predict" test: Which variable do you already know (or set) that you would use to predict the other? That known/set variable is X. The predicted variable is Y.
⚠️ Common mistake: Reversing X and Y. While some statistical measures (like r) are symmetric, the slope, y-intercept, and regression equation are completely different when X and Y are swapped. Always assign X and Y before entering data into Art of Stat.

The answer

Yes — conceptually, a residual is the vertical gap between a data point and the regression line. More precisely:

Residual = Observed y − Predicted ŷ   or   e = y − ŷ

Plug in the actual x-value into the regression equation to get ŷ (the predicted value). Then subtract that from the actual y-value from your data. The difference is the residual.

Positive residual: The actual point is above the regression line (reality was better than the model predicted).

Negative residual: The actual point is below the regression line (reality was lower than the model predicted).

Zero residual: The point falls exactly on the line (perfect prediction — rare in real data).

🧠 Example: Your model predicts an exam score of 78 for a student who studied 5 hours. The student actually scored 83. Residual = 83 − 78 = +5. The model under-predicted by 5 points — the actual score is 5 units above the line.
Why residuals matter: The regression line is chosen to minimize the sum of squared residuals (SSE). Large residuals identify data points the model fits poorly — possible outliers or influential observations worth investigating.

The answer

No. Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship — it says nothing about why that relationship exists.

A strong correlation between X and Y could exist for several reasons:

1. X causes Y (what we hope we're finding, but can't confirm from data alone)

2. Y causes X (reverse causation — maybe higher exam scores motivate more future studying)

3. A third variable (confounder) causes both — e.g., ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated because hot weather drives both. Neither causes the other.

4. Coincidence — given enough variables, some will correlate by pure chance

The gold standard for causation: A well-designed randomized controlled experiment, where subjects are randomly assigned to conditions. Observational studies — including regression — can only establish association, never causation.
🧠 Language tip: In conclusions, use "is associated with," "is related to," or "predicts" — not "causes," "leads to," or "produces." Causal language is only appropriate after a true experiment.
⚠️ Common mistake: Seeing r = 0.92 and writing "X causes Y." A correlation of 0.92 is very strong — but it is still just an association. No correlation value, however close to 1, authorizes a causal claim from observational data.
